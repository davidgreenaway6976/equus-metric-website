Is my horse overweight? Weight vs body condition score

Equus Metric guide. Updated 20 October 2026.

A number on a tape or a scale tells you how heavy your horse is. It does not tell you whether that weight is the right shape. To judge whether a horse is too thin, too fat or about right, vets and nutritionists use a body condition score, a 1 to 9 rating you can learn to do yourself with your eyes and your hands.

The short version

  • Weight measures size. Body condition score measures fat cover.
  • The Henneke scale runs from 1 (very thin) to 9 (extremely fat). For most horses in light to moderate work, 4 to 5 is the target range.
  • Check six areas by looking and feeling, score each one, then average them.
  • Repeat it every two to four weeks and watch the trend, not a single score.

Why weight alone isn't enough

Two horses can weigh exactly the same and be in very different condition. One may be tall, well-muscled and lean. The other may be smaller-framed and carrying too much fat. Weight also moves with gut fill, pregnancy and growth, and a thick winter coat or a round belly can hide what is really going on underneath.

That is why condition scoring pairs so well with a weight estimate. Take the weight with the free horse weight calculator (our guide to measuring weight at home explains the tape), then check condition with your hands.

What the 1 to 9 scale means

The Henneke body condition scale was developed in the early 1980s and is still widely used. Here is how scores group together:

ScoreDescriptionIn practice
1 to 3ThinLittle or no fat reserve. Increase forage and recheck weekly. If there is no improvement in two to three weeks, involve your vet to rule out a medical cause.
4 to 5ModerateThe target range for most horses in light to moderate work.
6Moderate to fleshySlightly generous. Fine for many horses, but worth monitoring in easy keepers or horses prone to metabolic problems.
7 to 9Fleshy to fatFat is building over the ribs, loin and tailhead. Consider reducing concentrate feed, increasing exercise, and speaking to your vet about metabolic risk.

How to score your horse, step by step

  1. Stand your horse square on level ground, in good light, and look from the side and from behind first.
  2. Then use your hands. Score each region by feel, not just by eye. Press your flat hand and fingertips firmly, because a coat or a round belly can fool the eye.
  3. Score the six areas below from 1 to 9 and write each one down.
  4. Average the six numbers and round to the nearest half point. That is the horse's overall body condition score.

The six areas, and what you will find

The table shows what each area looks and feels like at three points on the scale: thin (about 3), ideal (about 5) and fleshy (about 7). Your horse may score differently in different areas, which is normal.

AreaThin, about 3Ideal, about 5Fleshy, about 7
NeckFaint structures, a thin and narrow look. Feels like a hard, narrow ridge with no cushion.Blends smoothly into the shoulder, with no bumps or hollows.Fat starts to deposit along the crest and sides. You feel a thick, wide ridge along the top.
WithersAccentuated and prominent. The bones feel like a sharp edge.Rounded over the bones, cushioned by a smooth layer under your hand.Fat starts along the sides, so the bony ridge is harder to feel.
ShoulderStructure accentuated, with a sunken hollow behind the shoulder bone.Blends smoothly into the body, with no harsh lines or deep hollows.Fat deposited behind the shoulder, filling the hollow.
RibsEasily seen. Fingers drop into the gaps between ribs.Not visible, but easy to feel the moment you press a flat hand gently on the side.Individual ribs can still be found with firm pressure, but fat fills the gaps between them.
Loin (back)Fat built up about halfway on the spine, which is still easy to feel.The back is flat and level, with no ridge and no ditch.A visible soft crease runs down the middle of the back.

Look out for a cresty neck and fat pads. Fat that collects along the crest of the neck or around the tailhead can be linked with metabolic problems and a higher risk of laminitis, even when the rest of the horse looks reasonable. If your horse is an "easy keeper" with a thick crest, talk to your vet.

What to do with the result

If your horse is too thin

  • Start with more good-quality forage, which is the foundation of the diet, before adding concentrates.
  • Make changes gradually over a week or two.
  • Recheck weekly. If condition doesn't improve in two to three weeks, ask your vet to look for a cause such as teeth, worms or illness.

If your horse is too heavy

  • Look first at high-energy hard feed and treats, and cut them back.
  • Review the grazing. Options your vet or nutritionist may suggest include limiting turnout time, a grazing muzzle, or strip grazing.
  • Add exercise gradually, in line with the horse's fitness.
  • Don't crash-diet a horse or cut forage drastically. Horses need to eat little and often, so get advice on a safe plan.

In both cases

Change one thing at a time and give it a few weeks before judging the effect. Take weight and score together every two to four weeks, and photograph your horse from the same angles in the same light, because photos reveal slow changes that are easy to miss day to day.
